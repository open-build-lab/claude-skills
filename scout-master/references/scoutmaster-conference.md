## 1. What the conference is for

A Scoutmaster conference is a friendly, encouraging conversation between the Scoutmaster (or an assistant Scoutmaster the Scoutmaster has chosen) and a Scout. It is a required step for every rank from Scout to Eagle.

It is **not** a test. Don't re-test skills that were already signed off. The goals are to:
- get to know the Scout and how Scouting is going for them
- celebrate what they have done
- talk about Scout spirit and living the Scout Oath and Law
- hear their ideas for the troop
- help them set goals for the next rank and beyond

A conference can also be held at other times, such as when a Scout is struggling, missing meetings, or starting a new leadership position.

## 2. Setting it up safely (Youth Protection)

- **No one-on-one in private.** Hold it in view of other adults or Scouts, for example at the side of the meeting room, or at a picnic table at camp. Others can see but not hear.
- **Online conferences** need another adult or a parent present on the call.
- Keep the door open or pick an open space. Don't sit in a closed room or a car with a Scout.
- Uniform is encouraged but not required.

## 3. Before you start: what to know about the Scout

If the user hasn't told you these, ask for them in one short question before writing the questions, because the conference should fit the Scout:
- **Rank they are completing** (for example, finishing First Class)
- **Age or grade**
- **Leadership position**, if any (needed for Star, Life and Eagle)
- Anything notable: new to the troop, recently got back from summer camp, has had trouble, has a big accomplishment

If the user wants the questions right away without details, give a general set for the rank and say how to adjust it.

Also check before the meeting: requirements are signed off in Scoutbook (or the handbook), and for Star and up, that the position of responsibility and service hours are complete.

## 4. How to run it

1. **Warm up** (2 to 3 minutes): something light, like school, hobbies, a recent trip.
2. **Look back** on this rank: what they enjoyed, what was hard.
3. **Scout spirit**: how they live the Oath and Law, in Scouting and at home or school. Ask for examples, not definitions.
4. **Troop life**: patrol, meetings, outings, what they would change.
5. **Leadership** (Star and up): how the position went.
6. **Look ahead**: goals for the next rank, merit badges, outings, leadership.
7. **Close**: congratulate them and explain the next step (board of review).

Ask open questions ("Tell me about...", "What was it like...") and listen more than you talk. Pick 6 to 10 questions in total, not every question on the list.

## 5. Questions for every conference

- What has been the best part of Scouting for you lately?
- Which point of the Scout Law do you find easiest to live? Which one is hardest?
- Tell me about a Good Turn you did recently.
- How does Scouting help you at school or at home?
- If you were the Senior Patrol Leader for a month, what would you change?
- What would you like to do next?

## 6. Rank-by-rank questions

The questions grow with the Scout. Early ranks focus on settling in and basic skills; later ranks focus on leadership, service and the future.

### Scout (joining rank)
Often the Scout's first sit-down talk with a leader, so keep it warm and short (10 to 15 minutes).
- What made you want to join a Scout troop?
- What are you most excited to do? Campouts, hiking, cooking, something else?
- Can you tell me what the Scout Oath means to you in your own words?
- Which point of the Scout Law sounds most like you?
- What does the patrol method mean? Who is your patrol leader?
- Do you know what the buddy system is and why we use it?
- Is there anything you're nervous about, like your first campout?
- Who can you go to in the troop if you have a question?
- Goal: Which Tenderfoot requirement would you like to work on first?

### Tenderfoot
- How was your first campout? What did you like, and what surprised you?
- What did you learn about setting up a tent or cooking with your patrol?
- Which knot or first aid skill do you think you would really use one day?
- What did you learn from the fitness requirement? Did your results improve?
- Tell me about your service project. How did it feel to help?
- How is your patrol getting along? Do you feel part of the team?
- What does "Be Prepared" mean to you now?
- Goal: Which outing coming up are you looking forward to for Second Class?

### Second Class
- You've camped a few more times now. What's your favorite thing to do on an outing?
- Tell me about using a map and compass. Was it hard?
- What did you learn about fire safety and cooking?
- What did you learn from the swimming or water safety requirements?
- Tell me about a time you helped a newer Scout.
- What do you know about the dangers of drugs, alcohol and tobacco? (Keep it light and non-judgmental.)
- How are you balancing Scouts with school and other activities?
- Goal: First Class is a big milestone. What will you need to work on?

### First Class
First Class means the Scout has the core outdoor skills. Celebrate it as a big step.
- First Class is a big achievement. How does it feel?
- Which skill are you most confident teaching another Scout?
- Tell me about planning and cooking a meal for your patrol. What went well, what went wrong?
- What did you learn from the orienteering course?
- You learned about first aid and emergencies. What would you do if a buddy got hurt on a hike?
- What did you learn about citizenship and your community?
- How have you grown since you joined?
- Have you thought about a leadership position? Which one interests you?
- Goal: Which merit badges do you want to start, including any Eagle-required ones?

### Star
Star is the first rank with a leadership position and merit badges, so shift the talk toward leadership and service.
- Tell me about your leadership position. What did you actually do?
- What was the hardest part of being a leader? What would you do differently?
- How did you help other Scouts advance?
- Which merit badges did you earn? Which one did you enjoy most and why?
- Tell me about your service hours. Which project mattered most to you?
- How do you show Scout spirit when no one is watching?
- What do the younger Scouts see when they watch you?
- Goal: What position would you like next, and what do you want to achieve for Life?

### Life
A Life Scout is a senior leader in the troop and on the way to Eagle. Talk like you would with a young adult.
- Looking back on your leadership position, what are you proudest of?
- How do you handle a Scout who isn't listening or isn't doing their share?
- How have you mentored younger Scouts?
- What does servant leadership mean to you?
- Have you started thinking about an Eagle project? What needs do you see in your school, faith group or community?
- Which Eagle-required merit badges do you still need? What's your plan to finish them?
- When do you turn 18? Let's work out a timeline together.
- How do the values of Scouting show up in your life outside the troop?
- Goal: What would being an Eagle Scout mean to you?

### Eagle (final Scoutmaster conference)
The last conference before the Eagle board of review. Make it reflective.
- Tell me about your Eagle project from start to finish. What did you learn about leading adults and other Scouts?
- What was the hardest moment of the project, and how did you handle it?
- Which experience in Scouting shaped you the most?
- Who helped you along the way, and how will you thank them?
- How will you live the Scout Oath and Law after you turn 18?
- What are your plans for the future (school, work, service)? How did Scouting prepare you?
- How will you give back to the troop or to Scouting?
- What advice would you give a brand new Scout?
- Prepare them for the Eagle board of review: what to expect, what to bring (statement of ambitions, project workbook), and to relax and be themselves.

## 7. Adapting by age

- **Ages 11 to 12 (new Scouts):** short and simple questions, lots of encouragement, 10 to 15 minutes. Ask about friends, fun and feeling welcome. Avoid abstract questions.
- **Ages 13 to 14:** ask for examples and reasons ("Why do you think that worked?"). Bring in leadership and helping younger Scouts. Watch for drop-off risk, since this is when many Scouts lose interest. Ask what would keep Scouting fun for them.
- **Ages 15 to 17:** treat them like young adults. Talk about values, the future, Eagle timelines, high adventure, and their role as troop leaders. Let them lead the conversation.

A Scout who joined older (for example at 14) and is at a lower rank: keep the rank questions, but use the older-Scout style, and ask about their goals and timeline.

## 8. Adapting by leadership position

Ask about the actual duties of their position, what went well, and what they'd change:

| Position | Ask about |
|---|---|
| Senior Patrol Leader | Running meetings and the PLC, working with the Scoutmaster, handling conflict, delegating |
| Assistant SPL | Supporting the SPL, training the youth leaders, stepping in when the SPL was away |
| Patrol Leader | Planning with the patrol, keeping members involved, representing the patrol at the PLC |
| Troop Guide | Helping new Scouts, teaching skills, making new Scouts feel welcome |
| Quartermaster | Keeping track of gear, checking it in and out, repairs |
| Scribe | Taking PLC notes, attendance, records |
| Instructor | Which skills they taught, how they knew Scouts learned it |
| Den Chief | Working with a Cub Scout den, being a role model |
| Historian / Librarian / Webmaster | What they created or organized for the troop |
| Chaplain Aide | Helping with Scouts' Own services and respecting different beliefs |
| Outdoor Ethics Guide | Teaching Leave No Trace on outings |

Useful questions for any position:
- What did your job mean to the troop?
- Did you ever not know what to do? What did you do then?
- What would you tell the next Scout who takes this role?

If the Scout's position time was weak (for example they held the title but did little), this is the place to talk about it kindly. The rules say a Scout who holds the position and wasn't removed or told otherwise generally gets credit, so discuss expectations early rather than surprising them at the conference. Tell the user to check the current Guide to Advancement for the exact rules.

## 9. If the Scout isn't ready yet

- A Scoutmaster conference can't be used to add new requirements or to re-test skills.
- If something is genuinely incomplete (a requirement not signed off, position time not finished), explain kindly what's left and agree on a plan with dates.
- If the concern is about behavior or Scout spirit, talk about specific examples, listen to the Scout's side, and agree on how to show improvement. Involve the parents if needed.
- Always end positively. Schedule a follow-up.
- If there is a disagreement about whether a Scout is ready, tell the user to check the Guide to Advancement for the Scout's options and talk with the troop's advancement chair.

## 10. Wrap-up and recording in Scoutbook

1. Congratulate the Scout and shake hands.
2. Explain the next step: the board of review, who will be there, and to wear the uniform if the troop expects it.
3. Agree on one or two goals and write them down.
4. Record the conference date in Scoutbook (the "Scoutmaster conference" requirement for that rank) and let the advancement chair know the Scout is ready for a board of review.
5. Keep private notes short and respectful. Don't record sensitive personal details.
